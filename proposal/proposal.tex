\section{Introduction}

Robot policies are increasingly built on top of large vision-language
models (VLMs). Vision-language-action (VLA) models such as
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15818}{RT-2},
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09246}{OpenVLA}, and
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.24164}{$\pi_0$} fine-tune a pretrained
VLM backbone to map camera images and a natural-language instruction
directly to robot actions. Inheriting internet-scale visual and semantic
knowledge allows a single generalist policy to follow open-ended
instructions, recognize novel objects and scenes, and transfer across
tasks and embodiments far better than policies trained from scratch on
robot data alone.

This capability comes at a substantial computational cost. VLA policies
impose memory, bandwidth, and power demands that exceed what is
available onboard most robots, yet closed-loop control requires low and
predictable latency that offloading to a remote server cannot guarantee.
OpenVLA requires approximately 15~GB in a 16-bit configuration, nearly
double the entire 8~GB shared memory of a
\href{https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/autonomous-machines/embedded-systems/jetson-orin/}{Jetson Orin Nano Super}.
Even the smaller $\pi_0$ contains approximately 3.3 billion parameters,
requiring about 6.6~GB for its 16-bit weights alone, before accounting
for inference buffers, perception, and control software.
% Even on a desktop RTX 4090, OpenVLA runs at only about 6 queries per
% second, while edge modules offer 4--10$\times$ less memory bandwidth.

requirements and edge hardware.

Prior work has shown that quantization is an effective way to close
this gap. On a Jetson AGX Orin,
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01034}{QAIL} reports that INT4 weight
quantization reduces OpenVLA's memory footprint from 15.2 to 4.0~GB and
its inference latency from 955 to 375~ms, an approximately $2.5\times$
% speedup with corresponding energy savings. These gains stem from the
% memory-bound nature of batch-size-one autoregressive decoding, which
% re-reads every model weight to generate each action token.
However, quantization also reduces task accuracy. In the same
evaluation, 4-bit AWQ lowers OpenVLA's average LIBERO success from
74.0\% to 70.8\%, and LIBERO-Long success from 50.8\% to 47.2\%.
% Quantization-aware fine-tuning recovers much of this loss (73.1\%), but
% any single fixed precision still commits the policy to one point on the
% accuracy--efficiency tradeoff for every state it encounters.
This leads to the central question of our project:
\begin{quote}
\emph{How can robot policies be deployed efficiently on compute-limited
edge devices while achieving both a high success rate and low latency?}
\end{quote}

Specifically, we propose learning a \emph{router} that, at each control
step, switches among quantized versions of the same policy based on the
complexity of the current state. Our hypothesis is that \emph{not all
states require equal precision}: moving the arm through free space
tolerates coarse actions, whereas grasping, insertion, and other
contact-rich phases demand precise ones. Routing easy states to
low-precision models and reserving high precision for critical states
should therefore reduce average latency and energy while preserving the
success rate of the full-precision policy.

Previous work on dynamic precision for robot policies relies on
heuristic routers.
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07904}{DyQ-VLA} switches activation
precision using a hand-designed motion score with fixed thresholds, and
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24148}{MotionVQ} switches between
coarse and fine weights based on a fixed motion threshold. Such
heuristics must be tuned by hand and use motion as a proxy for how much
precision a state actually needs. Instead, we propose a lightweight
router learned offline. We label states visited by the policy with the
estimated loss in performance from acting at each precision, train a
small classifier on cheap features (proprioception, action history, and
previous precision choices) to predict the cheapest acceptable
precision, and refine it with DAgger-style data aggregation so that
training states match those the routed policy visits. Because the
router operates on features that are already available, its overhead is
negligible relative to the savings from reduced precision.

\section{Problem Formulation}

% \subsection{Setup}

We model robot manipulation as a finite-horizon decision process with
state space $\mathcal{S}$, action space $\mathcal{A}$, transition
kernel $P$, reward function $r$, initial-state distribution $\rho_0$,
and horizon $T$.
The input to the policy at time $t$, which we write $s_t$, consists of
the camera image, proprioception, and the language instruction.
An episode $\tau = (s_0, a_0, \dots, s_T)$ receives return
$R(\tau) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} r(s_t, a_t)$. The reward may be sparse,
such as a binary task-success signal at the end of the episode, or
denser, such as progress toward the goal. We do not assume access to a
learned value function, since VLA policies are trained by imitation
rather than reinforcement learning.

Let $\pi_1$ be a pretrained full-precision VLA policy. Post-training
quantization of $\pi_1$ yields a family of frozen policies
$\{\pi_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ at bit-widths
$\B = \{b_1 > b_2 > \dots > b_K\}$, with $\pi_1$ serving as the
reference. Each policy has a per-call inference cost $c_k > 0$
(latency or energy measured on the target hardware), with
$c_1 > c_2 > \dots > c_K$.

A router $g_\phi(k \mid z_t)$ selects which policy to query at each
step from a cheap feature vector $z_t = f(s_t, a_{t-1}, k_{t-1})$,
such as proprioception, recent actions, and the previous precision
choice. The routed policy $\pi_\phi$ acts as
\begin{equation}
  k_t \sim g_\phi(\cdot \mid z_t), \qquad a_t \sim \pi_{k_t}(\cdot \mid s_t).
\end{equation}
Our goal is to maximize expected return while minimizing compute:
\begin{equation}
  \max_\phi \; J(\pi_\phi) - \lambda\, C(\pi_\phi),
  \qquad
  J(\pi_\phi) = \E_{\tau \sim \pi_\phi}\big[R(\tau)\big],
  \qquad
  C(\pi_\phi) = \E_{\tau \sim \pi_\phi}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} c_{k_t}\Big],
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \ge 0$ trades off return against compute.

\subsection{Estimating the Precision Gap}

Optimizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective} directly would require knowing how
a single low-precision decision changes the expected return,
which is unavailable without a critic. Instead, we treat the
full-precision policy as the reference behavior: a state tolerates low
precision if the quantized policy behaves like $\pi_1$ there. We
therefore define the \emph{precision gap} $\Delta_k(s)$ of policy $k$
at state $s$ as how much $\pi_k$ diverges from $\pi_1$ starting from
$s$, with $\Delta_1(s) = 0$. We consider two estimators, in increasing
order of cost and fidelity.

\paragraph{Myopic action divergence.}
Running all $K$ policies on the same input $s$ gives their action
distributions directly. For OpenVLA, an action is a sequence of
$J = 7$ discrete tokens $a = (a^1, \dots, a^J)$, so by the chain rule
the KL divergence decomposes exactly over tokens:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\Delta}_k^{\mathrm{KL}}(s) = \sum_{j=1}^{J}
  \E_{a^{<j} \sim \pi_1}\Big[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(
  \pi_1(a^j \mid s, a^{<j}) \,\Vert\, \pi_k(a^j \mid s, a^{<j}) \big)\Big],
\end{equation}
which we evaluate by teacher-forcing both models on the tokens
decoded by $\pi_1$. For policies with continuous actions such as
$\pi_0$, we use the expected squared distance between actions,
$\hat{\Delta}_k^{\ell_2}(s) = \E\big[\lVert a^{(1)} - a^{(k)} \rVert_2^2\big]$,
with shared sampling noise. This estimator costs one forward pass per
policy but ignores whether a deviation actually affects the outcome:
the policy can recover from a small deviation in free space but not
from a missed grasp.

\paragraph{Multi-step rollout divergence.}
To capture whether a deviation compounds or is corrected, we reset the
simulator to $s$, execute one action from $\pi_k$, and then follow
$\pi_1$ for $H$ steps, comparing against a rollout that follows
$\pi_1$ throughout with the same random seed:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\Delta}_k^{H}(s) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M}
  d\big(x^{(k)}_{m,H}(s),\, x^{(1)}_{m,H}(s)\big),
\end{equation}
where $x^{(k)}_{m,H}(s)$ is the end-effector and object state after
$H$ steps in the $m$-th rollout and $d$ is a distance on that state.
Running the rollouts to the end of the episode ($H = T - t$) and
comparing returns instead of states gives a direct Monte Carlo
estimate of the drop in expected return,
$\hat{\Delta}_k^{R}(s) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}
\big(R(\tau_m^{(1)}) - R(\tau_m^{(k)})\big)$,
at the highest cost. These estimators require resetting the simulator
to arbitrary states, which LIBERO supports.

\subsection{Learning the Router}

We train the router offline by supervised learning, using the
estimated precision gaps as labels
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:router}). Each iteration has a data generation
phase, which rolls out a policy and labels the states it visits, and a
learning phase, which fits the router to all labeled states so far.
The first iteration rolls out $\pi_1$; later iterations roll out the
routed policy $\pi_\phi$ and aggregate its states into the dataset
(DAgger-style). This matters because routing decisions change which
states are reached, so the router must be trained on the states it
will actually encounter.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Offline router learning}
\label{alg:router}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Quantized policies $\{\pi_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ with costs
    $\{c_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$, simulator, gap estimator $\hat{\Delta}$,
    episodes per iteration $N$, iterations $I$
\State $\mathcal{D} \gets \emptyset$
\For{$i = 0, 1, \dots, I$}
    \State $\pi_{\mathrm{roll}} \gets \pi_1$ \textbf{if} $i = 0$
        \textbf{else} $\pi_\phi$
    \Statex \textit{\quad Data generation phase}
    \For{$n = 1, \dots, N$}
        \State Roll out $\pi_{\mathrm{roll}}$ and record visited states
            $s_0, \dots, s_{T-1}$
        \For{each visited state $s_t$}
            \State Run all $K$ policies on $s_t$ and compute
                $\{\hat{\Delta}_k(s_t)\}_{k=1}^{K}$
            \State Compute label $y(s_t)$ and weight $w(s_t)$
                (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:label}--\eqref{eq:weight})
            \State $\mathcal{D} \gets \mathcal{D} \cup
                \{(z_t, y(s_t), w(s_t))\}$
        \EndFor
    \EndFor
    \Statex \textit{\quad Learning phase}
    \State Train $g_\phi$ on $\mathcal{D}$ by minimizing
        $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\phi)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss})
\EndFor
\State \Return $g_\phi$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Learning loss.}
Each state receives a hard label, either the precision with the best
tradeoff between gap and cost, or the lowest precision whose gap is
within a tolerance $\epsilon$:
\begin{equation}
  y(s) = \arg\min_{k} \big[\hat{\Delta}_k(s) + \lambda c_k\big]
  \qquad \text{or} \qquad
  y(s) = \max\{k : \hat{\Delta}_k(s) \le \epsilon\}.
  \label{eq:label}
\end{equation}
Misrouting is most costly at critical states, where the low-precision
policies diverge most from $\pi_1$. We therefore weight each state by
\begin{equation}
  w(s) = 1 + \alpha \max_k \hat{\Delta}_k(s),
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
and train the router with a weighted cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(\phi) = -\,\E_{(z, y, w) \sim \mathcal{D}}
  \big[\, w \, \log g_\phi(y \mid z) \,\big].
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
